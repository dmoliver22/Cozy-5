What we are hunting, in one breath

A Windfall Position is an idea where being first is the whole trick. Something just changed. Nobody has walked through the new door yet. It would take you a weekend and no money. And the first one through becomes the name people remember. The example we measure everything against is the first AI coloring books on Etsy in 2022. The idea was the magic. The people who did it were not smarter than you. They were just there first.

Being first is the point. A head start that keeps others out afterwards (what we used to call the "moat") is nice, but it is no longer required. Jobs, licences and government rosters stay in the archive because they are real, but they are not what we call unicorns.

Plain-English glossary

  • First. Nobody has made this yet. Not "nobody has made it well". Counted, not guessed: the searches we ran and what came back.
  • The door. The change that made the idea possible: a new tool, a new store, a new rule, a big launch. "Did the door just open?" means that change happened in the last few months.
  • The window. How long until the door is crowded, or a big company ships its own version. After a big-company launch it is weeks, not months.
  • People come looking. There is a place (a store search, Google, an app store) where people already type the thing you would make, and it would show them yours. You do not sell. You list.
  • A way to get paid. An obvious price, a storefront, an affiliate link, a subscription. If there is no way to charge, it is a hobby.
  • Head start. Whatever keeps the first one first after others arrive: reviews, rank, the category name. A bonus.
  • Why nobody has done it. The specific reason the idea is still sitting there. It looks like a toy. It is embarrassing. The people who know the tool and the people who know the market never talk. It is buried in a document nobody reads. With no reason, assume it is taken.
  • Idea. One thing we might build. Lead = worth a look. Being checked = being counted and challenged. Unicorn = clears the bar and survived a challenge. Dropped = checked and failed, with the reason written down so nobody repeats it.
  • Ground. A place we searched. Promising = ideas came out of it. Fully searched = nothing there, with the reason. Logged so nobody digs it twice.
  • Way in. A kind of place where first-mover ideas hide. The list is on "Where to look".
  • Wave. Millions of people getting a new thing at once. Waves create new behaviors, and each new behavior needs a page, a table or a guide that nobody has made yet.
  • Early warning. A sign a wave is coming before it is news.
  • Challenge. The button on every record. A second pair of eyes on our side: the strongest objection, what is missing, what would flip the verdict, and what new path opens.

The seven marks of the real thing

  1. The idea fits in one sentence. If it needs a pitch deck, a licence or a qualification, it is not this.
  2. Something just made it cheap or possible. A new tool, a new feature, a new store, a new rule, in the last few months. With a date.
  3. People are already looking for it somewhere that would show them yours.
  4. You are first, and you counted. Store listings, Google, Reddit, GitHub, Etsy, the trade press. Not a feeling.
  5. There is a reason nobody has done it. Usually: it looks silly, it looks small, or the two groups who would need to meet never do.
  6. It takes a weekend and no money.
  7. The first one in keeps paying. Reviews, rank, the category name. The bonus, not the requirement.

How an idea is scored

Eight questions, each 0 to 5: Are you first? Will people come looking? Costs nothing to start? Easy to get paid? Do they come to you? Did the door just open? Is there a reason nobody has done it? Does being first keep paying?

To be called a unicorn: "Are you first?" must be 4 or 5. "Did the door just open?" must be 3 or more. The total must be 30 or more. One reason must be named for why nobody has done it. And it must have survived a challenge. Never round up. If a score is low, write why.

How the hunt runs

  1. Check the archive first. Type the idea into "Has this been searched already?". If the ground is fully searched, do not dig it again. If it is promising with a loose thread, pull that thread.
  2. Log the ground before digging. Name it, say which way in you took, mark it "being dug". This is what stops two sessions doing the same work.
  3. Follow the rabbit hole and write down the turns. Read the actual rule, the actual store page, the actual code repo, the obituary, the shutdown notice. Each twist goes in the notes.
  4. Count before you believe. "Nobody has made this" is a number: the search you ran and what came back. Count on the surface where that market's builders actually publish, not just where it is easy to count.
  5. Log the result even when it is nothing. A dead end with the reason written down saves the next hunter a day.
  6. Score coldly, then press Challenge. Nothing is called a unicorn before a challenge.
  7. Set a date. Ship it or drop it by a day written down. After a big-company launch that date is inside two weeks.
  8. Leave a loose thread, then write the journal. What was tried, what moved, what the gut says next.

The standard

The best of everything, not the best of what one search happened to find. A so-so idea logged as a candidate is worse than an honest dead end, because it steals attention from the hunt. If a path is wrong, stop walking it, write down why, and turn.

The Challenge button is not there to fail things. It is a second pair of eyes on the same side of the table. A challenge that reopens a dead end or opens a new path has done its job as much as one that confirms a unicorn.

Reading a wave the method that found the Muse ideas

  1. Find something tens of millions of people just got, in the last 30 to 60 days. Confirm it with a number and a date.
  2. List the five things people are visibly doing with it in week three or four (Reddit, X, TikTok, the press, support forums). What they do, not what it can do.
  3. For each behavior, name the thing nobody has made: the "which companies accept it" table, the "what went wrong" log, the "which one should I buy" guide, the one-page explainer for the person on the other end of the call. Not another list of use cases. Lists get made in days.
  4. Count that it is missing. Write the search and what came back.
  5. Check that people would find it, and that there is a way to get paid.
  6. Set the ship-or-drop date. The window is the gap between the launch and the company's own built-in version. Weeks.
  7. Do it again every two weeks. A live wave produces a new behavior every week, and each one stays unmade for about a month.

When to drop an idea

  • You cannot say what changed, and when. Drop it, or send it back to lead.
  • You cannot say why nobody has done it. Assume someone has.
  • You counted and it already exists three or more times, or a "best X for Y" article exists. The door is closing.
  • It needs real money, or a licence or qualification that takes more than a few weeks.
  • It needs you to be good at something, know someone, or hustle. That is a job, not a windfall.
  • It is only here because it was the best thing one search found. That is not a reason.
  • Lane lock. No two hunts in a row in the same lane. If the last hunt was "AI makes X, sell it on Y", the next one goes somewhere else entirely. A hunt may come back with nothing. The most expensive mistake so far was a whole hunt spent in the lane where the last gold rush happened, which is the one place everyone has already looked.
  • A cheap, fast credential is not a head start. If anyone can get on the list in under 40 hours and 30 days, the list fills in weeks.

Lessons this archive has paid for

  • A short list of providers is not a small market. Count the customers, not the providers. Colorado minted 330 instructors for 502 students in month one.
  • If the buyer can use anyone in the state, an empty county is low demand, not free money.
  • Look one step before the new rule. The scarce thing in Colorado was not the new course but the old volunteer class it leaned on.
  • "Newly cheap" is not "newly possible". Check when the store FIRST accepted the thing, not when the tool got cheap. Spotify took AI audiobooks in February 2025; the September 2026 tool only made them cheaper, and the obvious titles were already taken.
  • A store that pays from a shared pool and has no topic search is not Etsy. Being first only locks in where a search ranking exists.
  • Count the people already there before you fall for the tool. Two of four "empty" spots in hunt 2 had 2026 entrants running the exact play.
  • An invite-only beta with thousands inside is not a head start. Kindle Translate had thousands of authors pressing the button before anyone outside noticed.
  • "No official list" is not "no list". Volunteer SEO sites pre-build pages for events that have not happened yet. Count the unofficial lists before scoring a guide or directory.
  • After a big launch, lists get made in days; judgement calls stay unmade for weeks. Ten Muse use-case lists appeared within a month. Zero per-company scoreboards, zero incident logs, zero buyer's guides.
  • The window after a big-company launch is the gap until its own built-in version. Meta's chief AI officer was posting the dollars-saved tally within a week. Ship in seven days or do not ship.
  • A ritual nobody searches for has no customers. Everyone names their Muse; nobody searches for a name first.
  • Tech shutdowns are watched. Every software death with scale had three funded "migrate from X" pages within a week. The orphans worth having are physical, unglamorous, and too small for the big players.
  • What a company intends shows up months early; the store itself shows up a day early. Muse was visible three to eight months out: a hit open-source project it copied, its model name reused as a brand with a domain handover, a code name leaking with a test end-date, the CEO's earnings-call language, an open-weights model, an official developer kit posted eleven days early, a court ruling that shifted liability to the user. The app-store signs fired one day before launch. Watch the company's GitHub and the app store's developer id mechanically.
  • A big-company wave makes rules within weeks, not months. When the company routes its own demand (ads, a messaging app), the "no more" moment (terms, protocols, trust products) came on days 12 to 28. Every idea on such a wave needs a date inside two weeks.
  • Build next to the "no selling" clause. Gadget tokens cannot be sold, so kits, cases, firmware and guides are the sellable layer. The agent has no identifying signature, so "does it work here" pages and merchant readiness are the layer. Skills have no store, so reviewed vertical packs are the layer. Find the clause that says "not for sale" and build beside it.
  • Count where that market's builders actually publish. Zero scoreboards in fourteen GitHub repos was the whole case for the Trump Accounts tracker. That market lives in newsrooms, think tanks, trade press, 529 sites and SEO affiliates, and ten of them had built it by September. A GitHub count only counts a GitHub market.
  • A new kit is not a new store. OpenAI posted a plugin kit on Sep 29 and it read like a brand-new, empty directory. The directory had existed since July with 4,000 listings, and plugins cannot sell software. Count the store and read the payment policy before scoring "first". How many repos use a kit says nothing about how many things are on the shelf.
  • A developer kit's crowd is developers. Meta's gadget kit had 1,800 stars and 370 forks in a week, and not one ordinary person anywhere asking which board to buy. Before scoring "will people come looking" on anything that needs compiling or flashing, count the ordinary buyers: Reddit threads, YouTube comments, "which one should I buy" questions. Stars are not shoppers.
  • Count the platform's own help center first. The robot-call page assumed Google's off-switch was "documented only by agency blogs". Google had an official page with the exact toggles and a report form. Open the platform's help center before counting anyone else.
  • Check whether the capability already existed under another brand. A famous voice company "adding Cantonese" read like a door opening. Microsoft had Cantonese voices for six years and Google had 34. A big name adding a feature is news; the feature being possible was not. Before scoring "did the door just open", ask who else could already do this.
  • The buyer you imagine must actually have the problem. The Cantonese pack was pitched at Hong Kong parents who speak native Cantonese and need no romanisation or synthetic reader. Write one sentence: who buys, and why they cannot already do it themselves. Test that sentence before anything else.
  • If the honest answer is "no", the affiliate rail is fake. The kid-watch table was to be paid by watch links, and the truthful table says "banned" in every bell-to-bell state. A reader who learns that buys nothing. An affiliate rail only works when the page's honest answer is "yes, buy this one".
  • First at a free reference page is not first on a shelf. Every "first" this week was a table, an explainer or a log. Those become citations, not income. The coloring-book moment needs a shelf with a search box and a price field. Score "easy to get paid" on the shelf, never on hope.
  • "Nobody would put their name on that" is a claim to test, not a reason. A think tank built the Trump Accounts tracker in January. Before scoring on stigma, search the people who do not share it.
